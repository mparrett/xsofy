A perf audit in four phases. From "we don't know where the time goes" through telemetry, attribution, profiling-at-the-metal, to two targeted patches that cut average turn cost forty percent and total allocations a quarter.

The hold-to-repeat probe two days earlier had told us the engine couldn't sustain twelve turns a second. Was that the engine, the input plumbing, the xterm canvas, or the bridge? Honest answer: we'd been guessing. This quest stops guessing and measures.

Act I ⊟

The lantern — telemetry first, opinions later

Two pieces of infrastructure had to land before any measurement was trustworthy. First, per-turn telemetry inside the game loop: System/currentTimeMillis around world/update-world and the subsequent render, fanned out through bridge/emit-perf (a JS bridge event on WASM) and dbg/log (a line in debug.log on native). One read of timing, two outputs.

Second, a headless replay harness — bench/replay.lg — that drives update-world with a fixed keystream across multiple trials. No render, no bridge, no xterm: just the engine. Three fixtures (:wait, :autoex, :descend); five trials each; per-turn ms tabulated as avg / p50 / p95 / max.

fixture n avg p50 p95 max :wait 237 5ms 4ms 10ms 12ms :autoex 287 4ms 4ms 9ms 15ms :descend 265 6ms 7ms 11ms 11ms

Numbers swing run-to-run (cold start vs warm GC), so the harness reports across trials. Variance is the methodology's companion, not its enemy.

One subtle finding from the harness alone: native engine cost (5ms/turn) is much lower than what the live WASM build reported via the same telemetry channel (~196ms held-WAIT). The 30–40× gap is the WASM stack tax — exec + bridge + xterm + SAB busy-wait — which is interesting on its own but not the question for today. Stays for a follow-up.

Act II ≡

The seventy percent — which subsystem inside update-world?

The five-millisecond total per turn is a sum of six things: the player's action, then five threaded sub-phases — run-until-player-turn (other entities act), fire/step-fire (sparse fire-cell tick), burning-entities-ignite, drift-water-items, update-fov.

A second hook — a *perf-detail* flag in xsofy.world — gated currentTimeMillis markers around each step and stashed deltas on the returned world as :perf-detail. Phase 1 turned the flag on, drove the same fixtures, and read the breakdown:

fixture total rupt fire ignite drift fov+other :wait 7ms 5ms 0ms 0ms 0ms 1ms (71% rupt) :autoex 6ms 4ms 0ms 0ms 0ms 1ms (67% rupt) :descend 6ms 4ms 0ms 0ms 0ms 1ms (67% rupt)

run-until-player-turn — abbreviated rupt here — owns sixty-five to eighty percent of the engine's time. Fire, ignition, water-drift round to zero at millisecond granularity. FOV is in the noise.

A definite signal. The plan said "if one subsystem ≥ 40% of total, skip to optimization." We had 70%.

Worth flagging: this proportion is the *fresh-floor* case. Deeper floors with more entities and more lit fire cells will shift the numbers — fire-sim scales with effects-map size, rupt scales with entity count. The headline result still holds, but the relative weighting on level 9 will look different than on level 1.

Act III ▤

Beneath the script — Go pprof: what does the metal actually do?

Knowing which subsystem isn't the same as knowing which line. The let-go runtime is a Go-hosted bytecode VM; profiling the Go process tells us which functions underneath the source actually burn cycles. Six lines in let-go/lg.go added -cpuprofile and -memprofile flags wrapping the script-run path.

flat flat% sum% cum cum% 1.73s 16.68% 16.68% 5.49s 52.94% vm.(*Frame).Run ← bytecode dispatch loop 1.51s 14.56% 31.24% 1.51s 14.56% runtime.kevent ← GC / scheduler 0.84s 8.10% 39.34% 0.84s 8.10% runtime.pthread_cond_wait 0.80s 7.71% 47.06% 0.80s 7.71% runtime.madvise ← memory release back to OS 0.35s 3.38% 50.43% 0.38s 3.66% vm.(*Frame).push (inline) 0.17s 1.64% 64.42% 0.19s 1.83% runtime.ifaceeq 0.15s 1.45% 65.86% 0.21s 2.03% vm.hashValue 0.14s 1.35% 68.66% 0.34s 3.28% vm.(*hmapBitmapNode).find

The picture this paints is the opposite of what most optimizers expect to find. There is no algorithmic hot loop. Every cycle is uniformly distributed across VM machinery: about 22% in bytecode dispatch, about 38% in Go runtime and GC, about 7% in equality and hashing, about 5% in persistent-map node ops. No specific xsofy function or let-go stdlib primitive in the top thirty.

This re-frames the optimization question. If a profile finds one function at 60%, you optimize that function. If the cost is uniformly distributed across VM machinery, you have to reduce the volume of work that flows through that machinery. Fewer ops per turn. Fewer allocations per turn.

The heap profile pointed to where the volume came from:

23.09% vm.(*Frame).Run (any alloc during interp) 16.61% vm.(*hmapBitmapNode).nodeSeq ← map iteration seq nodes 13.87% vm.NewArrayVector ← vector creation 6.66% vm.(*ArrayVectorSeq).Next ← vector iteration 5.65% vm.(*PersistentMap).entries 4.44% vm.Keyword.Invoke ← (:kw m) calls allocate per call 4.28% vm.(*MapSeq).Next … 20.46% vm.(*PersistentMap).Assoc (cumulative)

Three patterns explain most of the allocations: iterating persistent maps allocates seq nodes; calling a keyword as a function ((:pos entity)) allocates a wrapper; every assoc-in chain walks the spine and allocates new internal map nodes.

Act IV ◧

Two patches, forty percent — allocation-reduction, where the volume came from

Two surgical changes in xsofy/world.lg, both on paths reachable from run-until-player-turn. Each touches one function. Each replaces one of the patterns named in Act III.

Patch 1 — rewrite next-actor. The old version picked the entity with the lowest :ticks via a vals → filter → filter → vec chain followed by a reduce. Four intermediate sequence/vector objects per call. Called roughly ten times per turn.

(let [actors (->> (:entities world) (vals) (filter #(or (:ai %) (= :player (:id %)))) (filter #(some? (:pos %))) (vec))] (when (seq actors) (:id (reduce …))))

The rewrite collapses to one reduce over the map's values with the predicate inline, and switches (:kw e) to (get e :kw) in the body:

(let [best (reduce (fn [best e] (if (or (nil? (get e :pos)) (and (not (get e :ai)) (not= :player (get e :id)))) best (let [t (or (get e :ticks) 0) bt (when best (or (get best :ticks) 0))] (cond (nil? best) e (< t bt) e (and (= t bt) (= :player (get e :id))) e :else best)))) nil (vals (:entities world)))] (when best (get best :id)))

Patch 2 — coalesce add-ticks. The old version did three nested updates to the same entity, each one walking the [:entities entity-id …] path independently and allocating fresh internal map nodes:

(-> world (update-in [:entities entity-id :ticks] #(+ (or % 0) cost)) (assoc-in [:entities entity-id :body :hp] new-hp) (assoc-in [:entities entity-id :regen-ticks] remainder))

One update-in to the entity, three field-updates internal to it. Same result, one spine walk:

(update-in world [:entities entity-id] (fn [e] (-> e (assoc :ticks (+ (or (get e :ticks) 0) cost)) (assoc-in [:body :hp] new-hp) (assoc :regen-ticks remainder))))

Act V ◌

What's left in the seam — the patterns repeat; the wins should compound

The two patches in Act IV applied templates, not one-offs. Both templates appear elsewhere in the codebase, on hot paths we didn't touch this session:

The ->> vals filter filter vec pattern shows up in find-nearest-enemy (called from fire-bow and the auto-target step), autoexplore-step's entity-aware predicates, and a few smaller spots in ai.lg. Each instance is a candidate for the same single-pass reduce rewrite.

The nested assoc-in chain pattern shows up everywhere a function modifies multiple fields on the same entity — combat (HP, status effects, ticks all in one turn), spell evaluation (multiple field changes to the ctx-passed entity), inventory churn (pickup/drop/equip both modify the player). Coalescing each costs one update-in.

The (:kw m) pattern is the easiest and the broadest. Idiomatic Clojure prefers it for readability. In let-go's current implementation it allocates per call. In a tight loop, every site is a quick swap. In warm code it's a wash. The discipline: get in hot loops, keyword-as-fn in everything else.

Next session's experiment: apply the same patterns to one more hot iteration (proposed: find-nearest-enemy + adjacent entity scans in ai.lg) and re-bench. If the second pass yields another twenty-thirty percent, the pattern compounds. If it yields less, we know the first-pass wins captured most of the structural problem and subsequent fixes are diminishing returns.

What we learned

Two layers of instrumentation — one in the game (per-turn ms, emitted to two outputs), one in the runtime (Go pprof on lg) — were enough to turn "is the engine slow?" into "the engine is slow because it allocates ~60M small objects per bench run, dominated by map iteration in one function called ten times per turn, and here's the patch." That's the entire arc.

  1. Subsystem attribution before profiling. If we'd gone straight to pprof, the answer "VM dispatch is 22%, GC is 38%" would have told us nothing actionable. Knowing the cost was concentrated in run-until-player-turn turned the same profile into specific call-site guidance.
  2. The profile won't always name an algorithm. A flat distribution across VM machinery is what you see when the work is *generic operations on persistent data structures*. The lever isn't a faster algorithm; it's fewer allocations and fewer ops, full stop.
  3. Idiomatic Clojure has hidden costs in let-go. (:keyword map) allocates a wrapper per call. (seq m)-based iteration allocates seq nodes. Every assoc-in chain re-walks the spine. Each of these is the right code for clarity — and the wrong code in a per-turn hot loop.
  4. One-line bench reveals the answer. The whole audit was driven by lg bench/replay.lg: ten seconds, three fixtures, before-and-after deltas. The harness existed before any patch; the harness made every change measurable.
  5. Two patches is more than two-percent. Forty percent off the headline number from seventy lines of diff. The compound interest of touching the right functions, sized correctly, in the right loop, is wild. The next pass might do as well; it might not. We'll know in another half hour.
